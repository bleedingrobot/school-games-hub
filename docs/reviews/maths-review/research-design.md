# Research: how to design maths games for ages 5 to 8

For: James (Douglas Park School). Used to score the seven Foundational games on the 8 criteria in BRIEF.md.
Written 7 Nov 2026. Plain English. Sources are at the end (numbered, e.g. [S3]). Only sources I actually found in a web search are listed. Where I am working from general knowledge rather than a source I checked, I say so.

How to read "evidence strength":
- **Strong** = many studies or a big review, replicated.
- **Moderate** = good studies, but mostly with older children or adults, or the effect size varies.
- **Limited** = few studies, or the claim is more a design idea than a tested result.

An honest warning first: most of the classic learning-science studies below used older children or university students. For 5 to 8 year olds the evidence is thinner. Where I stretch a finding down to young children I say so.

---

## 1. Intrinsic integration (maths IS the game)

**What it says.** Habgood and Ainsworth built a maths game (Zombie Division, for ages 7 to 11) in two versions. In one, the maths was the way you played (you used division to hit zombies). In the other, the same maths was a quiz between game levels. Children learned more from the integrated version, and in free time chose to play it about 7 times longer [S1]. The idea: a "sugar-coated quiz" (answer a question, then get a game reward) teaches less and is less loved than a game where the core action is the maths.

**Evidence strength.** Moderate. One well-known pair of studies, ages 7 to 11, replicated in spirit by many design papers but not a huge body of randomised trials. Directly relevant to our games.

**What a game should do.**
- The core action (the thing the child does most) should be the maths itself, for example hopping a frog along a number line to skip count, not answering a question to unlock a mini-game.
- Rewards and fun moments should follow from doing the maths well, not sit beside it.
- You cannot win by tapping at random or by skipping the maths.

## 2. Cognitive load, clarity and split attention

**What it says.** Working memory is small, and smaller in young children. Anything that makes a child think about the screen, rather than the maths, steals from learning [S2]. Two well-known fixes from multimedia research [S3]:
- **Split attention:** do not make a child look back and forth between a picture and its label. Put words/numbers next to the thing they describe.
- **Modality:** spoken narration plus a picture works better than lots of on-screen text plus a picture. For pre-readers this matters even more.
- **Redundancy:** do not show the same words on screen AND read them aloud if the child can already follow one. (For early readers, text with read-aloud on request is a sensible middle.)
- Also: one new thing at a time, no decoration that competes with the maths.

**Evidence strength.** Strong in the lab for adults and older children (Sweller, Mayer). Moderate for 5 to 8 year olds, because there is less direct testing, but the logic carries over strongly (young children have even less working memory).

**What a game should do.**
- One question, one clear action on screen at a time.
- Instructions given by voice (and short pictures), not paragraphs of text.
- Labels, numbers and pictures sit together; the moving or changing thing is where the child is looking.
- Cut decoration, timers and distractions that are not part of the maths.

## 3. Scaffolding, worked examples and how to fade them

**What it says.**
- **Scaffolding.** The word comes from Wood, Bruner and Ross (1976), who watched adults help children build a puzzle. Good tutors simplified the task, kept the child on goal and controlled frustration [S4].
- **Contingency rule.** Wood and colleagues' later "contingent tutoring" idea [S5]: when the child struggles, give MORE help straight away; when the child succeeds, give LESS next time. Help is matched to what the child just did, not fixed.
- **Worked examples.** Novices learn better from studying a solved example than from being thrown into problems [S2] (the "worked example effect").
- **Faded worked examples.** Start with a complete example, then an example with one step missing, then two, then a full problem. This move from example to independent work helps learning, especially for near transfer (similar problems) [S6].
- **Expertise reversal effect.** The help that helps a beginner can HURT a child who already knows the idea, because it adds work they no longer need [S7]. So support must fade as skill grows, and come back if skill drops.

**Evidence strength.** Strong for worked examples and the expertise reversal effect (many studies, mostly older learners, mostly maths and science). Moderate for fading with young children. Contingent tutoring is well supported in tutoring research, less in app research.

**What a game should do.**
- Offer a model (ten-frame, number line, part-whole picture) when the child is new or stuck.
- Make help come on automatically after a wrong answer or a long pause (not only if the child finds a hint button; many 5 year olds never will).
- Remove the model gradually as the child gets several right (for example the ten-frame dots disappear, then the frame, then only the number).
- Bring the model back after repeated errors.
- Do not keep showing the same help to a child who is clearly past it.

## 4. Feedback quality

**What it says.**
- Feedback is among the strongest influences on learning, but it can help or harm. Praise, rewards and punishment have weak effects. Feedback that says where the child is going, how they are going and what to do next works best, and best of all when it is about the task and the strategy, not the child [S8].
- Shute's review [S9] separates **verification** ("right/wrong") from **elaborated** feedback (why, a hint, a worked correction). Elaborated feedback usually works better for hard tasks. It should be timely but not so instant that the child stops thinking, avoid giving away the whole answer straight away, and avoid being controlling or critical. (Detailed guidelines here are from secondary summaries of the paper; I could not confirm them in the full text.)
- Praise for being "clever" can leave children less willing to try after failure than praise for effort [S10] (this study used 10 to 12 year olds; it is a limited fit for 5 to 8 but the direction is widely accepted).

**Evidence strength.** Strong for "feedback matters, type matters" (Hattie's 0.79 average effect is a large-scale synthesis, but those big averages mix very different studies). Moderate for elaborated beating verification, since it depends on the task.

**What a game should do.**
- After a mistake, show what went wrong using the same model the child is working with (for example, count the frame: you have 7, three more makes ten).
- Say it in voice and picture, short and kind.
- Let the child try again, with the help available, rather than just showing the answer.
- Praise effort and strategy ("you counted carefully"), not "you are so smart".
- Keep right/wrong sounds small; the maths is the reward.

## 5. Spaced, mixed and retrieval practice

**What it says.**
- **Spacing:** practice spread over days beats the same amount crammed in one go. Best gap depends on how long you need to remember [S11].
- **Retrieval practice:** trying to recall or work something out (rather than re-reading or re-watching) improves long-term memory [S12].
- **Interleaving/variety:** mixing problem types in practice helps children choose the right method, and beat "blocked" practice in a maths test study [S13]. (That study used university students on geometry; a later review found most textbooks have almost no mixed practice.)
- Early on, a new idea still needs a short blocked run so the child can see the pattern, before mixing.

**Evidence strength.** Spacing and retrieval: strong (hundreds of experiments), mostly older learners and word tasks. Interleaving in maths: moderate. For ages 5 to 8: limited direct evidence, but nothing suggests it works differently, and short sessions make spacing across days natural for a school game.

**What a game should do.**
- Bring back earlier skills now and then (for example a few "old" questions in each session).
- Mix question types once a child has the basics (for example mix bonds to 10 and bonds to 100 only after each is secure).
- Ask the child to work it out, not recognise it from a list of three obvious choices.
- Do not make a session rely on one repeated skill for 20 minutes.

## 6. Mastery learning and adaptive difficulty

**What it says.**
- **Mastery learning:** do not move on until the idea is secure. In software this is often done by tracking a mastery estimate per skill (Bayesian knowledge tracing, Corbett and Anderson [S14]).
- **Zone of proximal development:** tasks should be just beyond what the child can do alone but doable with help. (Vygotsky's idea; I did not look up a source for it.)
- **The 85% rule:** Wilson and colleagues showed, in computer models of learning and simple two-choice tasks, that learning is fastest when the learner is right about 85% of the time (about 15% errors) [S15]. Press coverage and the authors are careful: this has NOT been shown to be the right setting for real classroom children. Treat it as a useful target zone (roughly 75 to 90% success), not a law.

**Evidence strength.** Mastery learning and adaptive practice: moderate to strong in intelligent tutoring research (older children). 85% rule: limited for children (theory plus simple tasks), but it fits older experience that too easy is boring and too hard is discouraging.

**What a game should do.**
- Track success per skill, not just overall.
- Move up after steady success (for example 4 or 5 in a row), step down after repeated mistakes, so most children land around 75 to 90% right.
- Never let a child sit stuck at 30% right or bored at 100% right for long.
- Let a teacher see which level each child is working at.

## 7. Motivation: autonomy, competence, relatedness, and rewards

**What it says.**
- **Self-determination theory** [S16]: motivation is healthiest when three needs are met: **autonomy** (some choice, I am doing this, not being made to), **competence** (I can get better and I can see it), **relatedness** (connection to others/characters/class).
- **Overjustification:** a meta-analysis of 128 experiments found that tangible rewards for doing an activity reduce interest in it afterwards [S17]. Verbal praise did not undermine as much in that work. In games: points, coins and stars handed out for just turning up or tapping can turn a fun activity into a chore.
- **Growth mindset language:** effort and strategy praise, "not yet" language, mistakes shown as normal [S10]. Evidence for school-wide mindset programmes is mixed overall, so keep this to small, cheap choices in wording.

**Evidence strength.** SDT: strong and broad (not specific to games). Reward crowding-out: strong for tangible, expected rewards on already interesting tasks; weaker where the task was dull to begin with. Mindset wording: limited to moderate.

**What a game should do.**
- Give real choices (which path, which friend to help) without making them a burden.
- Show visible growth in skill (levels, "you can do bigger numbers now"), tied to maths done well.
- Use collection/rewards as a decoration of success, not a payment for tapping.
- Keep shared or classmate features optional and kind (no shaming leaderboards for 5 year olds).
- Write mistakes as "have another look" and "not yet", not "wrong".

## 8. Design for ages 5 to 8

**What it says.**
- **Pre-readers:** many 5 to 6 year olds cannot read instructions. Use voice, icons, animation of the action, and a replay-instructions button. Read-aloud audio takes load off working memory (modality [S3]).
- **Touch targets:** big, well-spaced, forgiving of imprecise taps and fat fingers. I did not find a citable number in my search; a common rule of thumb is at least about 44 by 44 CSS pixels for any adult, and noticeably larger (aim 64 or more) for young children. This is general practice, not a checked source.
- **Attention span and session length:** young children's sustained attention is short. Game sessions around 5 to 10 minutes with natural stopping points suit the age group (a widely used design rule of thumb; I did not locate a single strong source).
- **Consistency:** the same button always in the same place; same sounds mean the same thing.
- **Don't punish slowness:** timers add anxiety and stop thinking in children who count on fingers or with objects.

Hirsh-Pasek and colleagues [S18] review what makes children's apps educational for ages 0 to 8, and concentrate on four pillars:
1. **Active** (the child is thinking, not swiping to watch things happen),
2. **Engaged** (attention stays on the learning, not on distracting extras),
3. **Meaningful** (it connects to things the child knows or cares about),
4. **Socially interactive** (talk with others, characters, or in class; ties to a real adult).
Also needed: a clear **learning goal**. An app with the pillars but no goal is just entertainment.

**Evidence strength.** Pillars: a respected review, but a framework rather than a trial. Pre-reader design: strong common sense, moderate evidence. Session length and touch size: practitioner knowledge.

**What a game should do.**
- Every instruction available by voice, replayable.
- Big, forgiving, well-spaced buttons; no tiny close icons.
- No timers or speed pressure in early games.
- Short sessions with a clear "that's a good place to stop".
- Hook to classroom talk (for example a teacher prompt: "how did you know?").

## 9. Learning analytics: what to record for teachers

**What it says.** For a teacher, "score: 80" is nearly useless. What helps is, per child and per skill: how many attempts, how many right, which wrong answers (the specific mistake tells you the misconception), what level they reached, how much help they needed, and when. A mastery estimate per skill (even a simple "last 10 attempts" figure) beats a total score [S14]. Teachers also need an easy view that flags children who are stuck or not playing.

(This section is mostly design reasoning from knowledge-tracing research rather than studies of teacher dashboards; I did not find a strong source on dashboards for 5 to 8 year olds.)

**Evidence strength.** Limited for classroom dashboards. Moderate for per-skill mastery modelling.

**What a game should do.**
- Save per child: skill, level, attempts, correct/incorrect, the actual wrong answer chosen, hint use, date, and time spent.
- Roll that up into a simple per-skill status (new / practising / secure) a teacher can read at a glance.
- Flag "stuck" (many attempts at same level, low success) and "not played in 2+ weeks".
- Keep it per child and per class, not just a global score.

## 10. Evidence on maths apps for young children

- **Griffith et al. (2020, Pediatrics)** is a systematic review (not a meta-analysis, despite how it is sometimes described) of apps for children under 6. It found evidence of learning benefit from interactive apps for early academic skills, especially early maths, but called the evidence "emerging", and risk of bias was unclear in many studies [S19].
- **Outhwaite et al. (2017, Computers & Education)** tested a tablet maths intervention in UK primary schools. It found immediate and sustained gains, most for the lowest-achieving children, and no difference by first language or family income. The authors credit a well-designed curriculum and age-appropriate, interactive and repetitive-but-engaging activities [S20]. I did not verify the later 2019 Outhwaite paper, so it is not cited.
- **Hirsh-Pasek et al. (2015)** [S18]: most apps sold as "educational" do not meet the four pillars; learning is best when the app is part of a supported learning context.

Takeaway: well-designed maths apps can help, especially lower-achieving children, but the best results come from apps with a clear curriculum, adaptive practice, and a teacher or adult linking it to classroom learning. Quality varies hugely, which is exactly why a review like this is useful.

**Concrete models.** Many early-maths teachers rely on ten-frames, number lines and part-whole pictures. A review cited by a secondary source (Fyfe and colleagues, 2014) found evidence mostly favours moving from concrete to more abstract representations, and that manipulatives help but do not guarantee learning [S21, secondary only]. I found no direct trial evidence on ten-frames or number lines as such, so for those the support is teaching practice, not proven by a source I checked.

# The checklist (25 items) and rubric

Tick each item for each game. Items are grouped under the 8 criteria in BRIEF.md.

## Maths fidelity
1. The maths action IS the game (intrinsic integration), not a quiz in between. [S1]
2. You cannot win by guessing, random tapping or tapping everything; the maths is needed.
3. The representation shown (ten-frame, number line, part-whole) matches the idea being taught and is mathematically correct. [S21]

## Clarity
4. A pre-reader can tell what to do without an adult: voice instructions, replayable, pictures. [S3]
5. One question and one action at a time; no competing decoration; labels sit next to what they describe (no split attention). [S2, S3]
6. Big, forgiving touch targets; consistent button positions; no timer pressure; sessions short with a natural stopping point. [S18]

## Support and structure
7. A model (ten-frame, line, counters) is available when the child is new to a skill.
8. Help appears automatically after mistakes or a pause; the child need not hunt for a hint button. [S4, S5]
9. A worked example or demonstration is shown the first time a new type of question appears. [S2, S6]

## Fading
10. Support is reduced step by step as the child succeeds (for example dots, then frame, then number only). [S6]
11. Support returns if the child starts failing (contingency rule). [S5]
12. A child who clearly knows the skill is not slowed down by help they no longer need (expertise reversal). [S7]

## Progression
13. Difficulty builds in the order research and curriculum suggest (small numbers before big, concrete before abstract, one idea before mixing).
14. Difficulty adapts per child, aiming for roughly 75 to 90% success. [S15]
15. Older skills come back now and then (spacing and retrieval), and question types are mixed once each is secure. [S11, S12, S13]
16. Move-up needs steady success (mastery), not one lucky answer. [S14]

## Feedback
17. After a mistake the child sees what went wrong and why, using the same model they were working with. [S8, S9]
18. The child gets another go with help, rather than just seeing the answer.
19. Praise is about effort and strategy; mistakes are "not yet", never shaming. [S8, S10]

## Progress recording
20. The server saves per child, per skill: attempts, correct/incorrect, level, help used, time, date. [S14]
21. The wrong answer actually chosen is saved, so a teacher can spot the misconception.
22. A teacher can see at a glance who is stuck on what, and who has not played. [S14]

## Engagement
23. Fun comes from the maths action itself; rewards decorate success rather than pay for tapping. [S1, S17]
24. The child has real choices and visible growth (autonomy and competence); any social features are optional and kind. [S16, S18]
25. There is a clear learning goal and active thinking (not watching animations); a teacher could talk about it in class ("how did you know?"). [S18]

## Rubric: what 1, 2, 3, 4 look like

### 1. Maths fidelity
- **1:** The maths is wrong, misleading, or the game can be won with no maths (random tapping works).
- **2:** The maths is correct but is a bolted-on quiz, or guessing often works.
- **3:** Maths is correct and central; guessing mostly fails; one or two loopholes.
- **4:** Maths is the mechanic itself; representations are right; no way to win without the idea. (Items 1 to 3 all met.)

### 2. Clarity
- **1:** A 5 to 7 year old needs an adult to start; text-heavy, tiny targets, confusing screens.
- **2:** Some voice or pictures, but instructions unclear, too many things on screen, or hard to retry hearing them.
- **3:** Most children can start alone; voice available; a few cluttered or fiddly moments.
- **4:** Pre-readers play straight away; replayable voice; one thing at a time; large forgiving targets; no timer pressure. (Items 4 to 6 met.)

### 3. Support and structure
- **1:** No models or help at all.
- **2:** A hint or model exists but is hidden, optional, or only helps after the answer is shown.
- **3:** Models and help present at the right moments, mostly automatic; maybe no worked example for new types.
- **4:** Model, automatic help and a demonstration for new skills, all matched to the maths. (Items 7 to 9 met.)

### 4. Fading
- **1:** Support never changes (always on or always off), or removed suddenly.
- **2:** Support changes only by level jumps, or never comes back.
- **3:** Support fades with success OR returns with struggle, but not both, or only for some skills.
- **4:** Support fades gradually in steps with success and returns when the child struggles, per skill. (Items 10 to 12 met.)

### 5. Progression
- **1:** Random or fixed difficulty; no logical order.
- **2:** A sensible fixed order, but the same for every child; stuck children stay stuck, quick ones are bored.
- **3:** Sensible order plus some adaptation (for example step up/down by streaks).
- **4:** Research-ordered levels, adapts per child toward about 75 to 90% success, requires mastery before moving on, revisits and mixes old skills. (Items 13 to 16 met.)

### 6. Feedback
- **1:** No feedback, or feedback is misleading or shaming.
- **2:** Right/wrong only (verification), or the answer is simply shown.
- **3:** Some elaborated feedback (a hint or reason) for some mistakes, kind tone.
- **4:** Every mistake gets a reason shown with the same model, a retry with help, and praise about effort and strategy. (Items 17 to 19 met.)

### 7. Progress recording
- **1:** Nothing saved on the server (or only a total score).
- **2:** Totals saved (stars, levels reached) but no per-skill, per-attempt detail.
- **3:** Per-skill attempts and correct/incorrect saved, so a teacher could work out who is stuck with some effort.
- **4:** Per-skill, per-level data including the wrong answers chosen and help used, plus a clear teacher view of stuck and inactive children. (Items 20 to 22 met.)

### 8. Engagement
- **1:** Children avoid it, or only tap through for rewards.
- **2:** Fun is mostly decoration or points; maths is something to get past.
- **3:** Fun is mostly from playing the maths, with some extrinsic rewards; limited choice.
- **4:** Children choose to play it because the maths action is satisfying; real choices, visible growth, kind social features, a clear learning goal. (Items 23 to 25 met.)

---

## Caveats to remember when scoring
- Research was mostly done on older children; direction is clear, exact numbers (such as 85%, session length, touch size) are guides, not rules.
- A game that scores 4 on every line is not required. A 5 to 7 year old game that is clear, honest maths with good help and kind feedback (3s) is already better than most apps.
- When you score, prefer what you SEE the game do (code line, screenshot) over what the plan doc says it should do.
