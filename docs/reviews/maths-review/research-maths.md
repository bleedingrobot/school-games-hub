# Research: the maths ideas behind the Foundational games

For James (Year 4-6 teacher, Douglas Park School). Plain English. Research helper write-up for the fundamental maths review. No code was changed.

## Part 1. The seven ideas, one at a time

### 1. Subitising (seeing "how many" without counting)

**Two kinds (Clements 1999).** *Perceptual* subitising: instantly knowing 1 to about 4 without any counting. *Conceptual* subitising: seeing a bigger amount as groups you already know (a domino 8 as 4 and 4; a ten-frame with a full top row and 3 more as 5 and 5 and 3 = 13). Clements argues conceptual subitising is what feeds addition and place value.

**Real understanding vs rote.**
- Real: the child can say "it's 7" and explain "I saw 4 and 3" (or "a full row of 5 and 2"). They can see the same number in different layouts (line, dice pattern, scattered, ten-frame).
- Rote: the child memorises one picture (the dice-5 pattern) and cannot recognise 5 in a new layout; or counts quietly in their head and the "speed" is just fast counting.

**Learning order / NZ placement.** Perceptual 1-3, then 4-5 (Years 0-1, Stages 0-2); conceptual subitising of 5-10 using 5 and 10 as anchors (Years 1-2, Stages 2-4); groups of tens / structured arrays later (Years 2-4). It underpins Stage 4 to 5 movement (stop counting, start partitioning).

**Misconceptions / errors and what they tell you.**
- Off by one on 5 or 6: child is counting, not seeing groups. Needs structured, grouped arrangements.
- Right on dice patterns, wrong on scattered: pattern memory, not number sense.
- Gets small numbers fast but freezes above 5: no use of 5 as an anchor yet.
- Says the count of the screen "flash" but cannot say what they saw: attention, not maths; give more time or a slower flash.

**Best representations.** Dot cards and dice patterns, ten-frames (the 5-row structure is what makes 6-9 "5 and some"), rekenrek (two rows of 5 red/white beads), finger patterns. Move from structured to unstructured arrangements. Why: arrangement in groups invites seeing parts.

**Good practice / feedback.** Short (a few seconds), many repeats; always follow a flash with "How did you see it?" (explaining the grouping is the learning). After a mistake: re-show the same picture with the groups outlined or coloured ("5 and 2"), then let the child try a similar one.

**Implications for a game.**
- Display must be too short to count one by one (or numbers too large to count in the time), otherwise winning does not need subitising.
- Vary the arrangement, not only the number, so pattern memory fails.
- Reveal-after-answer should show the grouping ("I see 3 and 3"), not just the number.
- Do not reward speed alone; start with generous time and shorten as the child succeeds (fading).
- Include a "count on from a seen group" step to link to addition.

### 2. Counting and cardinality

**Real understanding.** Knowing the number words in order (stable order), matching one word to one object (one-to-one correspondence), and, the big one, **cardinality**: the last word counted tells you how many in the whole set (Gelman & Gallistel 1978). A child with real cardinality, asked "how many?" after counting, says the last number rather than recounting; and can "give me 6" and stop at 6 (Wynn's Give-a-number task). Beyond that: order-irrelevance (count the same set from any starting object), conservation (spreading the counters does not change the number), counting on (start from a known amount) and counting back.
Rote: reciting "one two three..." fast while pointing carelessly; cannot give 6 counters when asked; recounts from 1 after each addition.

**Learning order / NZ placement.** Number Framework: Stage 0 Emergent (can't yet reliably count a collection; lacks the number sequence and/or one-to-one matching), Stage 1 one-to-one counting, Stage 2 counting from one on materials, Stage 3 counting from one by imaging, Stage 4 Advanced counting (counts on and back; the end number of a count measures the whole set; links add/remove to forward/back sequences). Years 0-2 (typical). Counting to 10, then 20, then 100, then across decade boundaries (29, 30) is a hard step; counting beyond 100 goes into Years 2-3.

**Misconceptions and what they tell you.**
- Skips or double-counts objects: one-to-one matching not secure (needs touching/moving, or a tidy layout).
- Counts correctly but cannot say "how many": no cardinality yet (counting is a chant, not a measurement).
- "Last number is just the name of the last object": same problem.
- Recounts from 1 when one more is added: counting on not yet developed (Stage 2-3).
- Loses the sequence at the decade ("twenty-nine, twenty-ten"): pattern of the number names not learned.
- Teen/"-ty" confusion (thirteen vs thirty): sound-alike words; links to teen numbers (idea 6).
- Thinks a spread-out row has more: conservation not secure.

**Best representations.** Real objects the child can touch and move; counting strips and number tracks; hundreds chart; ten-frames; finger counting. Moving from counters to numerals to number line over time (CPA).

**Good practice / feedback.** Ask "how many?" after counting; ask to "give me N"; count forward and back from any number. Feedback on error: show the child's count as it happened (highlight each object as it was counted so a skipped or doubled one is visible), do not just say "wrong".

**Implications for a game.**
- The child must produce or confirm a count, not tap anything; "give exactly N" tasks (stop at N) make cardinality necessary.
- Objects need to be movable/markable (so the child can keep track) in early levels.
- Include "one more / one less", counting on from a shown number, and counting back.
- Voice counting-along (read-aloud) models the number names but should not do the counting for the child.

### 3. Part-whole and hidden amounts

**Real understanding.** A number can be seen as made of two (or more) parts, and the whole stays the same however it is split: 7 is 3 and 4, 2 and 5, 6 and 1. The child knows that if the whole is 8 and one part is 3, the hidden part must be 5 (and can say how they know), and that part + part = whole, whole - part = other part (the add and subtract links). This is Number Framework Stage 5 "Early additive part-whole": numbers are abstract units that can be treated as wholes or partitioned and recombined. Gray & Tall's "procept" idea fits: "3 + 4" is both a process (doing the adding) and an object (7 that can be re-split).
Rote: memorised "3 and 4 is 7" facts with no way to work out an unknown part; or always counting on from 1 to find the hidden part.

**Learning order / NZ placement.** Early partitioning of small numbers (2-5) with objects (Years 0-1); partitioning to 10 (Years 1-2); the missing-part (hidden) problems, "7 are shells, 3 are showing, how many are hidden" (Years 1-3); then partitioning with tens and ones (Years 2-4). Research summarised by Otago found the jump from Early additive to Advanced additive was beyond some children, so the later part-whole stages are bigger gaps than the early counting ones.

**Misconceptions and what they tell you.**
- Answers with the total or with the visible part ("there are 3"): does not distinguish whole from part. Needs the whole named and shown.
- Counts on from the visible part one by one: still a counting strategy (Stage 4); fine, but no part-whole recall yet.
- Treats "and" and "left" as the same operation, picks add when it should be subtract: has not linked part-whole to inverse operations.
- Thinks a different split changes the total: no conservation of the whole.
- Only knows one split per number: facts are isolated, not a connected family.

**Best representations.** Part-whole (part-part-whole) mat / diagram with whole on top and two parts below; ten-frames (the empty squares are the hidden part); cubes in two colours; bar model; number bonds house; "cover up" or hidden-counter tasks (Number Framework "hidden counters"). Why: a diagram makes the structure of whole and parts visible, and covered objects force the move from counting to reasoning.

**Good practice / feedback.** Always state the whole first ("There are 8 altogether"). Ask "how do you know?". After error: lift the cover and show the whole split into the two groups, then replay "3 showing + ? = 8" with the empty ten-frame squares coloured.

**Implications for a game.**
- The whole must be told (or visible) and the hidden part must NOT be countable on screen; the child works it out.
- Wrong answers should be handled by revealing and showing parts, not by trial and error (limit retries, or make guesses cost something).
- Mix: hidden part, hidden whole, and different arrangements, so the child connects add and subtract.
- Fade the diagram/ten-frame once the child is reliably right.

### 4. Bonds to 10 (and extended to 100 and 1000)

**Real understanding.** Knowing, without counting, the pairs that make 10 (1+9, 2+8, 3+7, 4+6, 5+5) and that each pair has a partner ("I know 7, so 3"). Understanding it as a *set of related facts* (if 6+4 = 10 then 4+6 = 10, 10-6 = 4) and spotting the pattern (one up, other down). Extension: the same idea scales: 30+70 = 100 is "3 tens + 7 tens = 10 tens" (units of ten); 300+700 = 1000 is hundreds. This is **unitising**: treating 10 as a unit, and then 10 tens as 100. The child should see 3 + 7 and 30 + 70 and 300 + 700 as "the same sum, bigger units", not as three unrelated facts.
Rote: chanting facts, but then cannot use them (e.g. cannot find 10 - 3 or 6 + 7 via 6 + 4 + 3).

**Learning order / NZ placement.** Bonds to 10 sit at the transition from Advanced counting (Stage 4) to Early additive (Stage 5), typically Years 1-2; nzmaths describes recognising compatible pairs to ten as essential for developing early part-whole strategies. Bonds to 20 (Years 2-3). Bonds to 100 using tens (and then those which need ones, e.g. 37 + 63) typically Years 3-4; bonds to 1000 (Years 4-5). Extension to decimals (0.3 + 0.7) comes later still (Year 5-6) (professional judgement on exact years).

**Misconceptions and what they tell you.**
- 10 = 6 + 5 style errors (off by one): counting without checking; has no visual of the ten.
- Gets 5+5, knows 1+9, unsure in the middle (3+7, 4+6): anchored on doubles only.
- Bonds to 100: answers 30 + 60 = 100 or 40 + 50 = 100: treating "9 + 1" (ones) logic wrongly; correct pair is 30+70 because 3 + 7 tens. Often means the child thinks of digits not tens as units.
- Bonds to 100 with ones (37 + ?): gives 73 (reverses digits) or 63 without checking the ones make 10: tens/ones logic not understood (need ones to make 10, then tens to make 10, so 3 tens + 6 tens + 1 ten for the carried one).
- Bonds to 1000 with 0s: loses place, 300 + 600 = 1000: same unit error.
- Mixes up "makes 10" with "adds to 10 more".

**Best representations.** Ten-frames (the empty squares ARE the partner), two-colour counters, rekenrek (slide 10 beads and see the split), Cuisenaire rods (10 = orange rod with partners), number line to 10 with jumps, "bonds house". For 100: place-value (tens) blocks or a ten-by-ten array, 100 on a bead string; for 1000 hundreds flats. Beanstalk-style "grow to the top" metaphors work as long as the child sees the amount, not just a number. CPA: objects -> picture/ten-frame -> number only.

**Good practice / feedback.** Quick-recall practice is valuable once understanding exists (fluency matters), but start with understanding. After error: show the ten-frame with the child's wrong guess and the leftover or overflow ("9 + 2 is 11, not 10: one too many").

**Implications for a game.**
- Must make the child supply the partner for a *shown* amount, with no near-zero-cost guessing (for example avoid 2-3 buttons where one is obviously right).
- Show the same structure at every scale: ones, tens, hundreds, with units labelled ("3 tens + 7 tens") so the link between 10, 100, 1000 is the idea, not the digits.
- Fade the ten-frame/rods and add time limits only after accuracy is high.
- Mix "make 10" with "10 take away" so the child sees the inverse.

### 5. Making ten and bridging ten to add (make-ten strategy)

**Real understanding.** To add 8 + 5, the child does not count on 5 from 8. They split 5 into 2 and 3, move 2 to fill 8 up to 10, and finish 10 + 3 = 13. This requires: bonds to 10 (how many to 10?), splitting the other number, and knowing teen numbers as "10 and some". nzmaths: "bridging to ten" depends on knowing the teen numbers and the facts to ten; the strategy is a Stage 5 part-whole strategy and it beats counting for larger numbers. Also 'through ten' for subtraction (13 - 9: 9 + 1 = 10, 10 + 3 = 13; or 13 - 3 - 6).
Rote: memorises 8+5 = 13 but cannot do 18 + 5 or 38 + 5 (the strategy does not transfer to bridging any decade). Real understanding transfers: 28 + 5 = 28 + 2 + 3 = 33.

**Learning order / NZ placement.** After bonds to 10 and teen numbers; Years 2-3 (Stage 4 to 5 transition), with extension to bridging any decade (Years 3-4). nzmaths lessons "Using Tens Frames for the Strategy of Bridging to Ten" and "Make a ten" show the sequence.

**Misconceptions and what they tell you.**
- Counts on from 8 by ones (8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13): still Stage 4 counting; works, but breaks down for bigger sums and is error-prone.
- Splits wrongly (takes 3 not 2 from the 5): bond to 10 is not secure.
- Fills to 10 correctly but then adds the wrong leftover (8+5: 8+2 = 10, then 10 + 5): did not subtract the 2 used. Does not track the split, so the whole is no longer conserved.
- Gets 10 + 3 = 13 wrong ("ten-three"): teen numbers not understood.
- Uses the strategy only for sums that "look like" a ten-frame (< 20): has not seen it as a general decade idea.
- Always makes ten from the smaller number: harmless, but better to bridge from the larger (less to move).

**Best representations.** Two ten-frames (one with the first number, one with the second); moving counters from the second frame to fill the first (physical or on screen, the child must make the move); rekenrek ("slide 2 beads to complete the row of 10"); an empty number line showing jumps (8 + 2, + 3); part-whole diagram showing the split of the second number. Why: seeing a full frame turns the problem into "ten and some", which the child already knows as a teen number.

**Good practice / feedback.** Ask "What do you need to make ten?" and "What's left?" Compare strategies ("counting on vs make ten: which is quicker for 9 + 6?"). After error: replay the child's move on the frames to show where the leftover went wrong.

**Implications for a game.**
- The child must perform both steps: (1) how many to complete the ten, (2) how many are left, then (3) the teen result. A game that asks only for the final total allows counting on, so it should check the split.
- Use numbers where counting on is painful (8 + 7, 9 + 6, 7 + 8) and also variants where the ten is not the next one (e.g. 28 + 5) to see transfer.
- Hide or shrink the ten-frame as the child succeeds.
- Do not accept only the final answer when the step structure is the point.

### 6. Teen numbers (ten and some more)

**Real understanding.** 13 means "one ten and three ones", not "one, three". The child can build 13 as a full ten-frame plus 3, say "ten and three", and knows that 13 is ten more than 3 and that 10 + 3 = 13. The number words hide the structure (thirteen, fourteen sound reversed or odd compared to "ten-three"), which is why English teens are hard (nzmaths notes this and uses ten-frames for teen numbers).
Rote: can count and read "13" but writes "31" or builds 13 as 13 loose counters one at a time.

**Learning order / NZ placement.** After counting to 10 and bonds to 10; usually Year 1-2 (Stages 2-4); then place value to 100 (Year 2-3), etc. Teens are the child's first encounter with place value (the 1 in 13 is a ten).

**Misconceptions and what they tell you.**
- Writes 31 for 13 (reversal), or "ten-three" mapped to 103: confuses the order of tens and ones; sound order (four-teen, but "twen-ty one") does not match writing order.
- Mixes up teen and "-ty" (13 vs 30, 14 vs 40, 15 vs 50): confuses the words; needs both words side by side with tens-ten frames.
- Counts all (1, 2, ... 13) to read a ten-frame plus 3: does not see the full ten as a unit.
- Says 11 and 12 as "one-teen, two-teen"? (they are irregular names): needs explicit teaching; no pattern to reason from.
- Thinks 10 + 3 needs counting on from 10: counting strategy, not place value; good to know but slower.
- Reads the digit 1 in 13 as "one" not "one ten": place value not grasped.

**Best representations.** Ten-frames (full frame = ten, second frame = some more); tens-and-ones blocks (a stick of ten and loose ones), bundle sticks; arrow cards (place value cards overlaid: 10 over 3 makes 13); number line from 10 to 20; bead strings (10 white then red). Keep the ten as a unit. Counting by 10 + ones aloud ("ten, ten and one, ten and two") before the standard names helps.

**Good practice / feedback.** Say both names ("thirteen is ten and three"); build, say, write; then read a written teen and build it. After error: place the child's number next to the correct build to show the mismatch ("this shows 31: three tens and one").

**Implications for a game.**
- The child must create or recognise "ten + n" (e.g. fill the ten-frame to 10 then add n) so the structure is built, not just the number named.
- Distractors should include reversed numerals (31 vs 13) and "-ty" confusions so winning needs place value.
- Include teen to ten and ones decomposition ("17 is ten and ...").
- Read-aloud should use both the standard name and the structure name.

### 7. Doubles and halves

**Real understanding.** A double is two equal groups (4 + 4); a half is one of two equal groups (half of 8 is 4); doubling and halving are inverses; and the facts become tools: near doubles (6 + 7 = 6 + 6 + 1), doubles to bridge (double 5 = 10, so 6 + 6 = 12), later doubling as x2 and halving as ÷2, and doubling a number by splitting into tens and ones (double 34: 60 + 8). Real halving requires *equal* shares; half of 9 is not a whole-number problem and leads to fractions (4 and a half).
Rote: knows 6 + 6 = 12 by recall but cannot use it to get 6 + 7, or thinks "half" means "split into any two parts".

**Learning order / NZ placement.** Doubles to 10 (Years 1-2, Stage 4-5), doubles to 20 (Year 2-3), near-doubles (Year 2-3), halves of even numbers (Years 2-3), doubling and halving as multiplication/division by 2 (Years 3-4), then doubling and halving to compute (Year 4-6), and fraction half (Years 1+ in shapes, Year 3+ in numbers). nzmaths includes a "Doubles facts" lesson that builds them with counters and ten-frames.

**Misconceptions and what they tell you.**
- Thinks double 5 is 10 but double 6 is 11 (adds 1): counting-on, not seeing two equal groups.
- Confuses doubling with adding 2 (double 6 = 8) or with squaring: does not see "equal groups".
- Half = "any two parts": equality of the two parts not understood.
- Cannot find half of an odd number: expected! shows when fractions/remainders are needed.
- Knows doubles but does not use near-doubles: facts remain isolated, not connected.
- Halving errors with 10s ("half of 70 is 5"): place value of the unit.

**Best representations.** Double ten-frames (mirror pattern; each side has the same counters); ladybird spots, dominoes and dice (the same on both sides); array "two rows"; mirror pictures (symmetry link); sharing counters into two equal groups for halving; number line with two equal jumps. Why: symmetry and equal-groups make the structure visible and link to multiplication and fractions.

**Good practice / feedback.** Ask "how do you know?" for near-doubles. After error: show the two groups side by side and make them equal; for halving, show the dealing out one for you, one for me.

**Implications for a game.**
- Pairing/matching mechanics (match a number with its double) risk becoming memory games; winning should need the child to compute or recognise the double, not memorise card positions (professional judgement).
- Include halving (inverse) and near doubles ("double plus one"), plus odd numbers for "can it be shared equally?".
- Show equal groups visually (mirror ten-frames) when the child errs.
- Escalate: doubles to 10 -> 20 -> tens (double 30) -> two-digit.

## Skip counting in 2s, 5s and 10s (idea 8)

**Real understanding.** Counting in equal jumps and knowing the jump is a *group size*: counting in 5s five times is 25 because five groups of five. The child can start at any number, go backward, and tell what number comes next without chanting from the start; and can link to multiplication (4 jumps of 5 = 4 x 5) and to patterns (all numbers in the 5s end in 0 or 5; all 2s are even; 10s are the next ten on a number chart). nzmaths: counting in twos, fives and tens is sensible early because the patterns are easy to remember and lead to divisibility rules; "Skip it to multiply it" uses skip counting to solve early multiplication problems.
Rote: reciting "2, 4, 6, 8, 10" like a song, but cannot say how many jumps were made, cannot start from 14, or says 35 after 25 in the 2s.

**Learning order / NZ placement.** Counting in 10s (using ten as a unit, linked to place value, Years 1-2), 2s (pairs, even numbers, Years 1-2), 5s (fingers, clocks, Years 1-3). Stage 4 Advanced counting strategies include counting in groups, Stage 5 uses them to solve multiplication; Years 2-3. Starting at non-multiples, backward counting, and counting in 3s, 4s etc. follow (Year 3-4), then to the times tables (Years 3-5).

**Misconceptions and what they tell you.**
- Can chant but cannot answer "what comes after 14 in 2s?": rote song, not a rule.
- Counts the *objects* in 5s but not the jumps (loses track of how many jumps): needs to track the unit count and the running total.
- Counts in 10s starting from a non-multiple of 10 (34, 44, 54) and loses the units: place value not connected.
- Errors at the decade (45, 50, 55 fine; 85, 90 ok but 95, 100 ... "ninety-ten"): sequence of number names.
- Jumps of 2 from an odd number: assumes you land on the same numbers (parity not understood).
- Counts a jump as the starting number (off by one on the number line): confuses "the number you land on" and "the jump".

**Best representations.** Number line with jumps drawn as arcs (shows both the jump and the landing); hundreds chart with multiples shaded (patterns); counting objects in groups (egg cartons, pairs of shoes, fingers); number strips and rekenrek (rows of 10 beads in 5s and 2s); bar/array models later (linking to multiplication). nzmaths unit "Number strips" and number-line peg activities (pegs gradually removed) support moving from physical to mental.

**Good practice / feedback.** Count forward and back from different starting points; stop and ask "how many jumps so far?"; predict ("what will we land on?"). After error: show the number line with the child's jump marked and the correct landing point, and point at the pattern in the hundreds chart.

**Implications for a game.**
- Winning must need *predicting* the next landing point (a missing number, or "will I land on 30?"), not tapping in order; avoid single correct path that can be traced by dragging along.
- Vary the starting number, direction and the step, not only 0 -> 100.
- Track number of jumps as well as the landing point; link to equal groups.
- Show the pattern (hundreds chart colours) on error, and fade the number line support.
- Distinguish 2s, 5s, 10s with different, meaningful contexts (pairs, fingers, tens) rather than only colours.

## Cross-cutting design principles (for the review)

1. **The idea must be necessary to win.** If the child can tap everything, guess one of two, or follow an arrow, the maths is optional. Check each game for a "no-maths" route to success.
2. **Representation, then fade.** CPA (concrete, pictorial, abstract): physical-style objects, then pictures like ten-frames, then numbers only. Support should step back when the child is right and return when they are wrong (this is review criterion 4).
3. **Feedback shows the maths.** After a mistake, replay the structure: re-show the frames or groups, highlight the missing or extra part.
4. **Explain thinking.** "How did you see it / know it?" is the research-backed core of subitising, part-whole and strategy work. Games can approximate with "which way did you do it?" choices or showing a worked example after the child answers.
5. **Fluency after understanding.** Speed is valid once the idea is secure (Number Framework Stage 5+ needs known facts), but earlier speed pressure rewards counting quickly or guessing.
6. **Strategy over fact.** The facts (bonds, doubles) are useful because they let you derive others (make ten, near doubles); games should link facts to strategies (Gray & Tall: flexible, proceptual thinking).
7. **Record per-idea, per-level evidence.** For a teacher to see who is stuck, per-child records need which idea and level, accuracy, hints used, and error types (e.g. "counted instead of subitised", "wrong leftover after making ten").

### What the table says in short (judgement)

- Highest-value early skills are counting and cardinality, subitising, part-whole, bonds, place value and equals-as-balance; all are well suited to digital games if the design forces the thinking.
- Foundational games already cover subitising, part-whole, bonds, teen numbers, make-ten, doubles and skip counting. **Possible gaps worth checking**: equals-as-balance and missing numbers, comparing/ordering and one more/less, place value beyond teens, money, simple graphs, and early fractions (halves).
- Skills least suited to a short digital game: real measuring (length, mass), data collection, and anything that depends on talk or physical manipulation.
